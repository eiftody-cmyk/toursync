The Yayoi Age in Japan
Parallel Histories of China and the Korean Peninsula

The Yayoi period (c. 1000 BCE – 300 CE) transformed the Osaka region from older Jōmon settlements into a landscape of rice paddies and metalworking villages — a shift that would eventually move the region's center of power onto the Uemachi Ridge.

Note: following recent research based on radiocarbon dating, this page uses an extended chronology for the Yayoi period, from around the 10th century BCE to the 3rd century CE. The arrival of rice farming and Yayoi culture varied by region.

Quick Answers

The Yayoi period (c. 1000 BCE – 300 CE) was a transformative era in Japan, marked by wet-rice agriculture, the adoption and production of bronze and iron tools, and growing social stratification and the formation of regional political powers — set against parallel developments in China and Korea.

What was the Yayoi period in Japan?

The Yayoi period (c. 1000 BCE – 300 CE) was a transformative era in Japan, marked by wet-rice agriculture, the adoption and production of bronze and iron tools, and growing social stratification and the formation of regional political powers.

Who was Queen Himiko?

Queen Himiko was a 3rd-century ruler of Wa in Japan. The Chinese history Sanguozhi (Records of the Three Kingdoms) records her as one who served the spirits (kido). After the Civil War of Wa she led the Yamatai confederacy, sending envoys to Wei in 238 CE, where she received the title 'Queen of Wa, Friendly to Wei' among other honors.

How did early Japan interact with China and Korea?

Ancient Wa received wet-rice agriculture, metal objects, and many other skills and goods through exchange with the continent via the Korean Peninsula. In the 1st–2nd centuries CE, Wa rulers sent envoys to the Chinese dynasties and built formal diplomatic relations.

Early Yayoi on the Kawachi Plain — The Osaka Evidence
At Nishifukui in Ibaraki, Early Yayoi pottery together with stone knives and clam-shell-edged stone axes has been excavated, confirming rice-farming communities on the Kawachi Plain. In Higashi-Osaka, the Guruido site preserves dwellings and square moated burials from the Yayoi period, and the Ikejima-Fukumanji site preserves production remains reaching back to the late Jōmon period. From around the 9th–8th centuries BCE in some areas, archaeology confirms wet-rice farming settlements in the Osaka region — and research increasingly seeks an earlier start to the Yayoi period than the traditional chronology allows.

Continue the Story at Osaka Castle

In the Yayoi period, wet-rice farming and metalworking techniques reached the archipelago via the Korean Peninsula, and Japanese society changed profoundly. The very ridge where Osaka Castle now stands changed along with it. Villages spread along the coastline, rivers, and lakes; new metal tools traveled with people on the move, and the balance of power between regions grew steadily wider.

If you’d like to follow how these Yayoi innovations set the stage for Osaka’s rise — from early settlements to the political struggles that defined the region — Before Japan had a Name traces 7,000 years of that story across the same ground where it unfolded.
