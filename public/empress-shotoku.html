Empress Shōtoku, Dōkyō & Naniwa — Power and Royal Authority
Historiographical Debates, Capital Shifts, and Official Chronicles

Empress Shōtoku first reigned as Kōken from 749 to 758 and again as Shōtoku from 764 to 770. Her era was shaped by the rise of the monk Dōkyō, Fujiwara no Nakamaro's rebellion, tensions over imperial succession, and capital moves that included Naniwa.

Quick Answers

Empress Shōtoku (718–770) ruled Japan twice — first as Empress Kōken (749–758), then again as Empress Shōtoku (764–770). She was the daughter of Emperor Shōmu and Empress Kōmyō and the first woman to become Crown Princess in Japanese history. Her reign saw the defeat of Fujiwara no Nakamaro's 764 rebellion, the extraordinary elevation of the monk Dōkyō, and the 769 Usa Shrine Oracle incident. Naniwa Palace mattered beyond its 744 capital move, as a key site for thinking about transport, diplomacy, and politics in the ancient state.

Who was Empress Shōtoku of Japan?

Empress Shōtoku (718–770) ruled Japan twice, first as Empress Kōken (749–758) and again as Empress Shōtoku (764–770). She was the daughter of Emperor Shōmu and Empress Kōmyō, and was made Crown Princess in 738, the first woman to hold that position in Japanese history.

What was the relationship between Empress Shōtoku and the monk Dōkyō?

After Dōkyō treated the retired Empress Kōken's illness at Hora Palace in Ōmi in 762, he rose rapidly at court and became Hōō (法王) in 766. Recent studies caution against reading this elevation simply as the familiar story that "Dōkyō sought the throne"; they also consider it in relation to Empress Shōtoku's own political and religious authority and the court institutions of the time.

What was the Usa Shrine Oracle incident of 769?

In 769 an oracle from the Usa shrine was presented as declaring that Dōkyō should ascend the throne. Empress Shōtoku sent Wake no Kiyomaro to obtain the deity's true response; according to the Shoku Nihongi, he returned with an oracle supporting succession by the imperial lineage and was punished for it.

Why did Naniwa matter during Empress Shōtoku's era?

Naniwa was a key transport and exchange point on the Inland Sea route and a gateway for diplomacy. Emperor Shōmu began building Naniwa Palace in 726 and moved the capital there in 744, and the palace's shifting fortunes track the court's struggle to balance continental trade, Buddhist institutions, and imperial authority.

Shōmu's Reign, Naniwa Capital & Princess Abe (724–749)
729 On Tour Imperial Proclamation
Elevation of Empress Kōmyō & The 729 Senmyō
Following Prince Moto's death (728) and the political restructuring after the Nagaya King Incident (729), Kōmyō became the first non-imperial consort elevated to Empress. The 729 imperial proclamation (senmyō) formally justified her position by invoking the ancient precedent of earlier empresses. Her elevation was anchored by her substantial economic and material resources inherited from Fujiwara no Fuhito, establishing a powerful new model for the empress's role.

Princess Abe Made Crown Princess

730–741 State Buddhism
Shared Religious Worldview & Buddhist Welfare
Both Shōmu and Kōmyō possessed a profound commitment to state Buddhism. From around 730, Kōmyō is said to have established relief facilities such as the Seyaku-in (dispensary) and Hiden-in for the sick and the poor — not as a pretext for her elevation, but as an expression of her piety. Such acts of Buddhist welfare are important for thinking about the bond between royal authority and Buddhism in the Nara period.

726–745 On Tour Capital Dynamics
Building Naniwa Palace & the Capital Moves
Emperor Shōmu began building Naniwa Palace in 726 and moved the capital there in 744. Naniwa was a key transport and exchange point on the Inland Sea, a gateway for diplomacy and the movement of goods. The court returned to Heijō-kyō in 745. Across Shōmu's reign the capitals moved repeatedly among Heijō-kyō, Kuni-kyō, Naniwa, and Shigaraki.

Kōken's Ascension & Dual Authority (749–762)
749–758 Court Politics
Nakamaro Dominates & Junnin Accession
Empress Kōken abdicated in 758 and Emperor Junnin succeeded her. Nakamaro received the name Emi no Oshikatsu and progressively higher offices, and his power grew accordingly.

762 On Tour Constitutional Crisis
Junnin and the Division of Authority
In 762, Empress Kōken declared that she would decide major matters of state and punishments, including appointments. The result was an unusual dual authority in which both the reigning emperor and the retired sovereign claimed political power.

762 Rise of Dōkyō
Dōkyō Treats Retired Empress Kōken
While at Hōra Palace in Ōmi, the retired empress Kōken fell ill. Dōkyō is credited with treating her illness; he then rose rapidly as a confidant of the retired empress.

749–762 Capital Stability
Heijō-kyō Center of Government
After the 744 move to Naniwa and the return to Heijō-kyō in 745, Heijō-kyō again became the principal center of court government. The political struggle increasingly centered on the distribution of authority between the emperor, the retired sovereign, and leading court factions.

Nakamaro's Rebellion, Concentration of Authority & Monastic Elevation (764–767)
764 Armed Conflict
Nakamaro's Rebellion & Deposition of Junnin
Nakamaro rose in Ōmi but was defeated by the imperial army and eventually killed. Emperor Junnin was deposed and exiled to Awaji.

764–766 On Tour Historiographical Debates
Concentration of Authority under Shōtoku
Following the suppression of Nakamaro's rebellion, Shōtoku concentrated political authority — including appointments and punishments — in the throne and removed or punished many of Nakamaro's supporters. It was an era in which the emperor herself strongly drove political decision-making.

765–767 Clerical Peaks
Dōkyō's Elevation & The Hōō-gūshoku
Dōkyō becomes 太政大臣禅師 in 765 and 法王 in 766. In 767, the 法王宮職 was established as an extra-statutory office supporting his household and administration, standing alongside the existing court administration.

764–767 Capital Control
Shōtoku's Political Authority
The defeat of Nakamaro removed one of the powerful political forces constraining Shōtoku's authority. She subsequently promoted trusted allies, including Kibi no Makibi, while drawing Buddhist institutions and ritual closer to the center of imperial government.

The Usa Shrine Oracle Incident & Imperial Succession (769)
769 Chronicles
Usa Shrine Oracle Incident & Wake no Kiyomaro
In 769, an oracle was delivered from the Usa shrine declaring that Dōkyō should ascend the throne. According to the Shoku Nihongi, Wake no Kiyomaro reported an oracle supporting succession by the imperial lineage; he was stripped of office, renamed 別部穢麻呂 (Wakube no Kitamaro), and exiled.

769 On Tour Historiographical Debates
Dōkyō's Throne Ambition Reconsidered
Later accounts portray Dōkyō as aspiring to the throne, but surviving contemporary sources do not allow us to confirm directly that he plotted a secular usurpation. Recent studies instead place greater emphasis on Shōtoku's own conception of imperial authority and the religious-political system surrounding her, and reassessments from this perspective continue to appear.

769 Monastic Stance
Dōkyō & Buddhist Authority
Dōkyō's appointment as 法王 was an extraordinary case of a monk holding a political and religious position extremely close to royal authority. How this relationship should be understood as a model of kingship remains a subject of scholarly debate.

769 Imperial Defense
The Oracle and Imperial Succession
The episode exposed a fundamental question: could a religiously authenticated claim alter the hereditary principle governing imperial succession? The Shoku Nihongi records that Kiyomaro returned with an oracle supporting succession by the imperial lineage.

Shōtoku's Death, Exile & The Historiography (770–794)
770 & 797 Official Records
Shōtoku's Death & Shoku Nihongi
Shōtoku dies childless in 770, and Kōnin — a son of Prince Shiki of the Tenji line — succeeds her. Completed in 797, the Shoku Nihongi presents Shōtoku's reign and the Dōkyō episode through the perspective of the court that emerged after her death, contributing to the later negative image of Shōtoku and Dōkyō.
Click to expand
770–Modern On Tour Historiographical Reassessment
Dōkyō's Exile & Female Sovereigns
After Shōtoku's death, Dōkyō was not executed but demoted to bettō of Shimotsuke Yakushi-ji, leaving for Shimotsuke in 770 and dying there in 772. At least, there is no surviving Nara-period law that explicitly banned female accession itself. The long gap after Shōtoku reflects the strengthening of a male-line, male-preferred principle of succession over the longer term; the next woman to reign was Empress Meishō in the Edo period.

784–794 Clerical Relocation
Capital Relocation & Buddhist Ritual
The court moved first to Nagaoka in 784 and then to Heian in 794. The moves reorganized the political and geographic relationship between the emperor, the capital, and the great Nara temples and the court, while Buddhism itself remained central to imperial ritual.
Click to expand
784–794 Urban Transformation
Abandoning Heijō-kyō for Heian
The court abandoned Heijō-kyō for Nagaoka in 784 and Heian in 794. These new capitals reorganized the geography of imperial government and helped establish a new political order. In Heian-kyō, the great Nara temples were set apart from the center of the capital, and relations between the court and the Buddhist temples were reorganized in a new form.

Sources & Further Reading

The main references for readers who want to explore this history further:

  • Primary Chronicle: Shoku Nihongi (続日本紀) — the basic source for the Nara period covering 697–791, completed in 797. In English, Ross Bender's Nara Japan, 749–770: A Study and Translation of Shoku Nihongi, Tenpyō Shōhō 1–Hōki 1 (4 vols., 2015–2016) covers the reigns of Emperors Kōken/Shōtoku (749–770), with a partial earlier translation by J.B. Snellen (1937).
  • Joan R. Piggott: The Emergence of Japanese Kingship (Stanford University Press, 1997) — how royal authority was built and contested in the Nara court.
  • Herman Ooms: Imperial Politics and Symbolics in Ancient Japan: The Tenmu Dynasty, 650–800 (University of Hawai'i Press, 2009) — the dynastic politics behind Kōken/Shōtoku's reigns and Dōkyō's elevation.
  • The Cambridge History of Japan, Vol. 1: Ancient Japan (Cambridge University Press, 1993) — scholarly chapters on the Nara court, capital shifts, and female sovereigns.
  • National Museum of Japanese History (Rekihaku): research and exhibition materials on state formation, royal authority, and ancient capitals (English site rekihaku.ac.jp).
