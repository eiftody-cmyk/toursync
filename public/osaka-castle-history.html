Osaka Castle
Toyotomi's Vision, Tokugawa's Erasure

The Tokugawa account, what scholars now think, what was lost or hidden, and who lived there

Quick Answers

Osaka Castle's story spans more than four centuries of Japanese history — from Toyotomi Hideyoshi's rise from peasant to ruler, through the two devastating sieges of 1614–1615, to the Tokugawa shogunate's rebuilding of the castle on a substantially altered plan. What stands today is not the castle Hideyoshi built, but the one the victors constructed on top of his buried foundations.

What is Osaka Castle?

Osaka Castle is a major Japanese castle in Chuo-ku, Osaka. Built by Toyotomi Hideyoshi in 1583 on the site of the Ishiyama Honganji fortress, it became a castle of unprecedented scale and splendour. After the Toyotomi clan's destruction in 1615, the Tokugawa shogunate rebuilt the castle, covering much of Hideyoshi's original foundations under new stone walls.

Who built Osaka Castle?

Toyotomi Hideyoshi began building Osaka Castle in 1583, using it as his political headquarters. The original castle was destroyed by the Tokugawa in 1615 and rebuilt between 1620 and 1630 under the second shogun, Tokugawa Hidetada, using architects Todo Takatora and Kobori Enshu. The Tokugawa main keep was completed in 1626.

What happened during the Siege of Osaka?

The Siege of Osaka consisted of two campaigns — the Winter Campaign (1614) and the Summer Campaign (1615). Tokugawa Ieyasu besieged the castle, which was held by Toyotomi Hideyori, Hideyoshi's young heir. In the Summer Campaign of May 1615, the castle fell. Hideyori and his mother Yodo-dono died, ending the Toyotomi clan and the Sengoku period.

Why did the Tokugawa bury Toyotomi's stone walls?

When the Tokugawa rebuilt Osaka Castle starting in 1620, they covered Hideyoshi's original stone walls and foundations under several metres of fill, building entirely new walls on top. The visible stone walls belong to the Tokugawa-period castle; Toyotomi-period walls survive beneath the later construction.

Toyotomi's Vision (1583–1598)
1583 Historical Record
Hideyoshi Builds Osaka Castle
Toyotomi Hideyoshi begins construction of Osaka Castle on the site of the Ishiyama Honganji fortress, which Oda Nobunaga had destroyed in 1580. The castle becomes the largest in Japan and the headquarters of Hideyoshi's unification of the country.

1583–1597 On Tour Archaeological Record
The Castle as Political Instrument
Modern scholarship reads the castle not merely as a fortress but as a deliberate political statement — "tenka" (all under heaven). Hideyoshi's rise from peasant to ruler was unprecedented in Japanese history, and the castle was designed to project that authority. The scale, the gold leaf, the Western-style turrets — all were calculated to demonstrate that a new kind of power had arrived.

1537–1598 Who Lived There
Toyotomi Hideyoshi
Born a peasant in 1537, Hideyoshi rose through the ranks to become Japan's most powerful ruler. He ruled from Osaka Castle from 1583 until his death at Fushimi Castle in 1598, using it as the seat of his government and the center of Momoyama culture — gold leaf, tea ceremony, and the Golden Tea Room.

Momoyama Culture
The castle was the center of Momoyama culture — gold leaf decoration, tea ceremony, and art patronage. The Toyotomi folding screen, now in Graz, Austria, is one of the most important surviving artifacts of this era. It left Japan and was never returned, a physical reminder of what was lost when the Tokugawa buried the Toyotomi legacy.

1569–1615 Who Lived There

1593–1615 On Tour Archaeological Record
The Castle as Seat of Government
The castle was not just a fortress — it was one of the principal political centres of Hideyoshi's regime. He ruled from here, issuing laws, conducting diplomacy, and managing the distribution of land. The castle's position on the Uemachi Plateau gave it control over the routes to the interior and Osaka Bay, the same geographic logic that had drawn every previous civilization to this site.

The Siege and Erasure (1600–1620)
1614 Historical Record
Winter Campaign
Tokugawa Ieyasu besieges Osaka Castle with approximately 200,000 troops. The castle is defended by Toyotomi Hideyori and his forces. After fierce fighting, a truce is negotiated — but it is a trap.
Click to expand
1614 On Tour Archaeological Record
Two Distinct Military Operations
Modern scholarship distinguishes the Winter Campaign from the Summer Campaign as two fundamentally different operations. The Winter Campaign was a siege with negotiated settlement; the Summer Campaign was a deliberate war of annihilation. The "peace" of the Winter Campaign was a deliberate feint — Ieyasu used the ceasefire to allow the outer moat to be filled, making the castle vulnerable for the final assault.

1614 What Was Lost
The Outer Moat
The negotiated ceasefire of the Winter Campaign included a clause requiring the Toyotomi to fill in the outer moat. This was not a peace agreement — it was a deliberate military strategy to weaken the castle's defenses before the Summer Campaign. The outer moat, which had protected the castle for decades, was deliberately destroyed.

1593–1615 Who Lived There
Toyotomi Hideyori
Toyotomi Hideyori, Hideyoshi's son and heir, was born at Osaka Castle in 1593. He was the last Toyotomi lord of the castle, defending it during both sieges. In June 1615, he committed suicide as the castle fell, ending the Toyotomi line.

June 1615 Historical Record
Summer Campaign — The Fall of Osaka Castle
Tokugawa Ieyasu launches the Summer Campaign with approximately 155,000 troops. The castle falls after fierce fighting. Hideyori and his mother Yodo-dono commit suicide. The Toyotomi clan is destroyed.

May 7–8, 1615 On Tour Archaeological Record
The Final Battle of the Sengoku Period
The Summer Campaign was the final battle of the Sengoku period — the last armed conflict of the civil wars that had torn Japan apart for over a century. The fall of Osaka Castle marked the definitive end of the Toyotomi clan and the beginning of 265 years of Tokugawa peace. The battle was fought on the grounds that now form Osaka Castle Park.

June 5, 1615 What Was Lost
Yodo-dono's Death
Yodo-dono died with Hideyori on 8 May 1615, shortly after Osaka Castle fell. Later accounts differ over the precise circumstances of their final hours, but the established historical account records their deaths on that date.

1615 Historical Record
The Castle Burns
Osaka Castle is burned to the ground during the Summer Campaign. The Toyotomi clan is destroyed, and the Tokugawa shogunate consolidates its control over all of Japan.

1615–1620 Archaeological Record
The Deliberate Burial
When the Tokugawa rebuilt Osaka Castle starting in 1620, they did not simply repair Hideyoshi's castle. They rebuilt Osaka Castle on a substantially altered plan, covering much of the Toyotomi-period remains with massive quantities of fill and building entirely new walls on top. The physical result was also a powerful political statement: the Tokugawa castle literally covered the castle of their defeated rivals.

1615 What Was Lost
Hideyori's Child
Hideyori had a son, Kokumatsu, and a daughter, Naa-hime. After Osaka fell in 1615, Kokumatsu was captured and executed in Kyoto, while Naa-hime was spared and eventually entered religious life. Their different fates marked the final destruction of the Toyotomi household.

1543–1616 Who Lived There
Tokugawa Ieyasu
Tokugawa Ieyasu (1543–1616) besieged Osaka Castle in 1614–1615 but never lived there. He used the castle as a military objective, not a residence. His grandson, Tokugawa Ieyasu's successor Tokugawa Hidetada, ordered the reconstruction of the castle starting in 1620.

Osaka Castle Rebuilt (1620–1945)
1620 Historical Record
Reconstruction Begins
Reconstruction begins under the second shogun, Tokugawa Hidetada. The old Toyotomi structures are dismantled. The project is entrusted to master castle architects Todo Takatora and Kobori Enshu. Daimyo across Japan are assigned to quarry and transport stones through tenka bushin (nationwide corvée).

1620–1628 On Tour Archaeological Record
Why Tokugawa Rebuilt Rather Than Destroyed
The decision to rebuild rather than destroy Osaka Castle was itself a political statement. By rebuilding on top of the buried Toyotomi foundations, the Tokugawa were asserting that they had not merely defeated the Toyotomi — they had replaced them. The new castle was a physical manifestation of the new order, built on the literal burial of the old.

1579–1632 Who Lived There
Tokugawa Hidetada
Tokugawa Hidetada (1579–1632) was the second shogun who ordered the reconstruction of Osaka Castle. He was the son of Tokugawa Ieyasu and the father of the third shogun, Tokugawa Iemitsu. The castle was completed during his tenure, around 1628.

1626 Historical Record
Tokugawa Main Keep Completed
The Tokugawa main keep (tenshu) is completed — a five-story exterior with eight interior levels, white plaster walls, and gold decorations. It is taller than Hideyoshi's original. The broader reconstruction of the castle was substantially completed by 1630. The castle is used for shogunate ceremonies and as the administrative headquarters for western Japan.

1628–1665 Archaeological Record
The Castle's Dual Role
The rebuilt castle served a dual purpose: it was both the shogunate's administrative and military headquarters for all of western Japan, and a symbolic statement of Tokugawa power. The castle housed a large garrison of samurai and stored gunpowder, treasure, and rice. In peacetime, the main keep served primarily as a storehouse.

1665–1868 What Was Lost
A Castle Without Its Tower
After the 1665 lightning fire destroyed the main keep, the castle continued to function for over 200 years without its iconic tower. The absence of the keep symbolized the castle's declining symbolic role — it was no longer the seat of a unified Japan, but a regional administrative center.

1620–1868 Who Lived There
The Osaka-Jodai
The Osaka-Jodai (Castle Governor) was a senior official appointed by the shogunate to oversee the castle and the administration of Osaka city. Exclusively filled by fudai daimyo (hereditary Tokugawa vassals), the jodai governed from the Nishinomaru area and worked alongside the Osaka machi bugyo (town magistrates).

Lightning strikes and burns down the main keep (tenshu). It is never rebuilt during the Edo period. The castle continues to function without its iconic tower for approximately 266 years.

1665–1868 Archaeological Record
The Castle Without Its Tower
The loss of the main keep did not diminish the castle's political importance. It remained the shogunate's western headquarters and a critical military and administrative center. The absence of the tower, however, symbolized the castle's declining symbolic role — it was no longer the seat of a unified Japan, but a regional administrative center.

1843 What Was Lost
Decades of Neglect
After decades of neglect, the bakufu collected money from the public to repair several turrets in 1843. The castle's physical deterioration reflected the broader decline of the Tokugawa regime, which would end just 25 years later with the Meiji Restoration.

1867 Who Lived There
Tokugawa Yoshinobu
Tokugawa Yoshinobu (1837–1913), the 15th and final shogun, took up residence at Osaka Castle in 1867. He was the only shogun to base himself outside Edo Castle during his tenure. He resigned as shogun in November 1867, and in February 1868, he fled Osaka Castle aboard the warship Kaiyo Maru as imperial forces approached.

1870 Historical Record
Osaka Arsenal Established
The Osaka Arsenal is established within the castle grounds. Military leader Omura Masujiro proposed building weapons production facilities at the castle, citing its central location and excellent transport links. The arsenal grows to become one of the largest military armories in the Japanese Empire, eventually occupying the entire eastern side of the castle grounds.

1870–1945 Archaeological Record
From Feudal Fortress to Military Factory
The transformation of Osaka Castle from a feudal fortress to a modern military arsenal was itself a political statement. The Meiji government was deliberately repurposing the symbols of the old regime for the new nation-state. By 1945, the arsenal employed over 60,000 workers and was one of the largest military production centers in the Japanese Empire.

August 14, 1945 What Was Lost
The Final Bombing
On August 14, 1945 — the day before Japan's surrender — 145 B-29 bombers attacked the Osaka Army Arsenal. More than 80 percent of the arsenal's facilities were destroyed. A bomb exploded beside the main keep on the northeastern side, buckling the stone foundations — damage still visible today.

1931 Who Lived There
The 1931 Keep
In 1931, the main keep was rebuilt in ferroconcrete, funded by public donations of 1.5 million yen raised by Osaka's mayor. This is the structure that stands today. The 1931 reinforced-concrete keep survived the bombing, although numerous historic structures around it were destroyed.

Today, Osaka Castle is no longer a residence but a museum and public park. The grounds are visited by millions of people each year. The Stone Walls — A Story in Stone

The stone walls of Osaka Castle are not just defensive structures — they are the physical evidence of a political act. When the Tokugawa rebuilt the castle starting in 1620, they covered Toyotomi Hideyoshi's original stone walls under several metres of fill, building entirely new walls on top.

Toyotomi Walls vs. Tokugawa Walls

Archaeological investigations show that the Toyotomi-period masonry differs markedly from the later Tokugawa walls. Toyotomi-period corner stones are elongated and irregular, while the Tokugawa walls employ much more regular cut stone. The visible stone walls belong to the Tokugawa-period castle; Toyotomi-period walls survive beneath the later construction.

The Octopus Stone

The famous Octopus Stone (Tako-ishi) belongs to the later Tokugawa castle, not to Hideyoshi's original walls. Its enormous size illustrates the extraordinary scale of the Tokugawa reconstruction, when daimyō from across Japan were mobilised through the system of tenka-bushin to rebuild Osaka Castle.

The Toyotomi Stone Wall Museum

The Toyotomi Stone Wall Museum, opened in 2025, allows visitors to see a section of the Toyotomi-period stone wall that archaeologists first identified in 1984 and subsequently excavated. For centuries, the surviving remains of Hideyoshi's castle lay beneath the later Tokugawa construction. The walls of Osaka Castle are not just stones — they are the physical evidence of who gets to write history.
