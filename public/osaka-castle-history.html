Osaka Castle
Toyotomi's Vision, Tokugawa's Erasure

The Tokugawa account, what scholars now think, what was lost or hidden, and who lived there

Quick Answers

Osaka Castle's story spans more than four centuries of Japanese history — from Toyotomi Hideyoshi's rise from peasant to ruler, through the two devastating sieges of 1614–1615, to the Tokugawa shogunate's rebuilding of the castle beginning in 1620. What stands above ground today — the keep and the stone walls — is the Tokugawa-period castle rebuilt after 1620. Beneath it, the Toyotomi-period castle lies buried under layers of earth.

What is Osaka Castle?

Osaka Castle is a major Japanese castle in Chuo-ku, Osaka. Toyotomi Hideyoshi began building it in 1583 on the site of the Ishiyama Honganji, and it became a castle of unprecedented scale and splendour. After the Toyotomi clan's destruction in 1615, the Tokugawa shogunate rebuilt the castle, covering much of the Toyotomi-period stonework and remains under new walls.

Who built Osaka Castle?

Toyotomi Hideyoshi began building Osaka Castle in 1583, making it one of the principal centres of his regime. The original castle was destroyed by the Tokugawa in 1615 and rebuilt between 1620 and 1629 under the second shogun, Tokugawa Hidetada, who entrusted the basic layout (katō) to the castle architect Todo Takatora. The Tokugawa main keep was completed in 1627.

What happened during the Siege of Osaka?

The Siege of Osaka consisted of two campaigns — the Winter Campaign (1614) and the Summer Campaign (1615). Tokugawa Ieyasu besieged the castle, which was held by Toyotomi Hideyori, Hideyoshi's young heir. In June 1615, the castle fell in the Summer Campaign. Hideyori and his mother Yodo-dono died, ending the Toyotomi clan and the Sengoku period.

Why did the Tokugawa bury Toyotomi's stone walls?

When the Tokugawa rebuilt Osaka Castle starting in 1620, they covered the Toyotomi-period walls and remains under massive quantities of fill, building entirely new walls on top. Research by the Osaka Castle Museum shows, through excavations, that the Tokugawa castle was built over the earlier Toyotomi works. The visible stone walls belong to the Tokugawa-period castle; Toyotomi-period walls survive beneath the later construction.

Toyotomi's Vision (1583–1598)
1583 Historical Record
Hideyoshi Builds Osaka Castle
Toyotomi Hideyoshi begins construction of Osaka Castle in 1583 on the site of the Ishiyama Honganji — the stage of the Ishiyama War. The temple complex burned after the Honganji withdrew in 1580, and its grounds became the foundation of the new castle. It becomes the largest castle in Japan and a headquarters of Hideyoshi's unification campaigns.

1583–1597 On Tour Archaeological Record
The Castle as Political Space
Modern scholarship reads the castle not merely as a fortress but as a political space that visualised Hideyoshi's power and the idea of "tenka" (all under heaven). Hideyoshi's rise — from a low birth, up through the ranks of the Oda camp to ruler — symbolises the upward mobility of the Sengoku period. The great scale, the gold-leaf tiles, and the Western-style turrets were important elements in projecting the authority of the Toyotomi regime.

1537–1598 Who Lived There
Toyotomi Hideyoshi
Born a peasant in 1537, Hideyoshi rose through the ranks to become Japan's most powerful ruler. From 1583 he oversaw the construction of Osaka Castle, making it one of the key centres of the Toyotomi regime, though his own seat of administration shifted over time — Kyoto's Jurakudai after he became kanpaku, and Fushimi Castle in his final years. It was also a centre of Momoyama culture — gold leaf, tea ceremony, and the Golden Tea Room.

Unification of Japan
Hideyoshi completes the unification of Japan that Oda Nobunaga began. Osaka Castle was one of the principal political and military centres of the Toyotomi regime, though Hideyoshi's own seat of administration shifted over time — Jurakudai in his years as kanpaku and Fushimi Castle in his final years. The castle grew as a symbol of Hideyoshi's authority and of the Toyotomi regime's monumental building projects.

Momoyama Culture
The castle was the center of Momoyama culture — gold leaf decoration, tea ceremony, and art patronage. The "Toyotomi-period Osaka Castle Screen," now held in Graz, Austria, is a valuable painted record of the Toyotomi-period castle and townscape. Its being held abroad shows that the cultural heritage of the Toyotomi period survives not only at today's Osaka Castle.

1569–1615 Who Lived There
Yodo-dono (Chacha)
Yodo-dono, also known as Chacha, was Hideyoshi's consort and the mother of his heir, Toyotomi Hideyori. She lived at Osaka Castle and was a woman of great political and social influence in the final years of the Toyotomi regime. Her place in the castle's story during the sieges of 1614–1615 is a central theme of the Goddess, Queen, Empress, Concubine tour.

1593 Historical Record
Birth of Toyotomi Hideyori
Hideyori is born at Osaka Castle in 1593 — a long-awaited son for Hideyoshi. After the infant Tsunayoshi's early death, he becomes the central heir of the Toyotomi house.

1593–1615 On Tour Archaeological Record
The Castle as Seat of Government
Osaka Castle was one of the key political, military, and ceremonial centres of the Toyotomi regime. Standing at the northern end of the Uemachi Plateau, it lay close to water transport along the rivers and toward the Seto Inland Sea, as well as overland routes toward Kyoto — the same geographic logic that had drawn civilisations to this site.

1598 What Was Lost
The Toyotomi-period Osaka Castle Screen
The Toyotomi-period Osaka Castle Screen — one of the most important surviving artifacts of Momoyama culture — is now held in Graz, Austria. It depicts the townscape of Toyotomi-period Osaka, with the castle, town, temples, and people, and is a valuable painted record of the Toyotomi-period castle and its surroundings. Its being held abroad shows that the cultural heritage of the Toyotomi period survives not only at today's Osaka Castle.

Nene (Kōdai-in)
Hideyoshi's wife, Kōdai-in (Nene), played an important role in the founding of the Toyotomi regime and in its political and personal networks. After Hideyoshi's death she was based in Kyoto, maintaining her ties with the Toyotomi family.

The Siege and the Fall of the Toyotomi Family (1614–1615)
1614 Historical Record
Winter Campaign
Tokugawa Ieyasu besieges Osaka Castle with approximately 200,000 troops. The castle is defended by Toyotomi Hideyori and his forces. After fierce fighting, a peace agreement is reached through negotiations between the Tokugawa and Toyotomi sides.
Click to expand
1614 On Tour Archaeological Record
Two Distinct Military Operations
Modern scholarship distinguishes the Winter Campaign from the Summer Campaign as operations of different character. The Winter Campaign ended in a negotiated peace; the Summer Campaign broke out after relations deteriorated further. The peace terms included filling the castle's outer moats and dismantling its fortifications, drastically reducing its defences — a condition that led directly to the Summer Campaign of 1615.

1614 What Was Lost
The Outer Moat
The peace terms of the Winter Campaign included filling the castle's moats and dismantling its fortifications, fundamentally changing its defensive structure. The outer moat and other works that had protected the castle for decades were filled in — as a result, the peace cost the Toyotomi side most of the castle's defensive strength.

1593–1615 Who Lived There
Toyotomi Hideyori
Toyotomi Hideyori, Hideyoshi's son and heir, was born at Osaka Castle in 1593. He was the last Toyotomi lord of the castle, defending it during both sieges. In June 1615, he committed suicide as the castle fell, ending the Toyotomi line.

June 1615 Historical Record
Summer Campaign — The Fall of Osaka Castle
Tokugawa Ieyasu launches the Summer Campaign with approximately 155,000 troops. The castle falls after fierce fighting. Hideyori and his mother Yodo-dono commit suicide. The Toyotomi clan is destroyed.

May 7–8, 1615 On Tour Archaeological Record
The Final Battle of the Sengoku Period
The Summer Campaign was the final battle of the Sengoku period — the last stage of the large-scale civil wars that had continued since the Sengoku era, and the point at which Tokugawa rule stabilised further. The fall of Osaka Castle marked the end of the Toyotomi clan and the opening of a long period of Tokugawa political order. The battle was fought on the grounds that now form Osaka Castle Park.

June 4, 1615 What Was Lost
Yodo-dono's Death
Yodo-dono is said to have taken her own life with Hideyori shortly after Osaka Castle fell on 4 June 1615. Contemporary and later records differ in their accounts, and uncertainty remains over the details of their final hours.

1569–1615 Who Lived There
Yodo-dono at the Castle
Yodo-dono was present at Osaka Castle throughout the sieges of 1614–1615 and was a central figure within the Toyotomi household. Her death during the Summer Campaign remains one of the most controversial episodes in Japanese history. The Goddess, Queen, Empress, Concubine tour covers her story in detail.

1615 Historical Record
The Castle Burns
Osaka Castle is burned to the ground during the Summer Campaign. With the destruction of the Toyotomi clan, the large-scale Toyotomi regime capable of opposing the Tokugawa shogunate ceased to exist.

1615–1620 Archaeological Record
The Toyotomi Castle Under Earthen Fill
When the Tokugawa rebuilt Osaka Castle starting in 1620, they did not simply repair Hideyoshi's castle. They rebuilt it on a substantially altered plan, covering much of the Toyotomi-period remains with massive quantities of fill and building entirely new walls on top. The Tokugawa-period castle was thus built over the Toyotomi works, which lie buried beneath layers of earth — today almost everything visible above ground at Osaka Castle dates from the Tokugawa rebuilding or later. This layered structure is key evidence for thinking about how the Tokugawa treated the Toyotomi legacy.

1615 What Was Lost
Hideyori's Child
Hideyori had a son, Kokumatsu, and a daughter, Naa-hime (later Tenshū-ni). After Osaka fell, Kokumatsu was executed in Kyoto, while Naa-hime was taken in as a ward of Chiyo-hime and entered Tōkei-ji temple in Kamakura. Their different fates marked the final destruction of the Toyotomi household.

1543–1616 Who Lived There
Tokugawa Ieyasu
Tokugawa Ieyasu (1543–1616) besieged and attacked Osaka Castle during the sieges of 1614–1615, though he never used the Toyotomi castle as his residence. His successor as second shogun, Tokugawa Hidetada, ordered the rebuilding of the castle starting in 1620.

Tokugawa Rebuilding of Osaka Castle (1620–1868)
1620 Historical Record
Reconstruction Begins
Rebuilding begins under the order of the second shogun, Tokugawa Hidetada. The old Toyotomi structures are dismantled. The basic layout of the castle (katō) is entrusted to the master castle architect Todo Takatora. Daimyo across Japan are assigned to quarry and transport stones through tenka bushin (nationwide corvée).

1620–1629 On Tour Archaeological Record
Why Tokugawa Rebuilt Rather Than Destroyed
The Tokugawa did not simply abandon Osaka Castle after the war — they rebuilt it as a massive fortress, fundamentally altering the moats and stone walls and constructing the new castle over the earlier Toyotomi works. The rebuilding was also a symbolic act: it demonstrated that the Tokugawa shogunate had brought the politically and militarily important city of Osaka under its own control.

1620–1629 What Was Lost
The Buried Walls
Toyotomi-period walls and fortifications were covered by massive layers of earth during the Tokugawa rebuilding, with new walls constructed on top. The visible stone walls belong to the Tokugawa-period castle; Toyotomi-period walls survive beneath the later construction. Underground walls believed to be Toyotomi-period were first identified in 1959, and in 1984 a large section was discovered beneath the honmaru; parts are displayed at the Osaka Castle Toyotomi Stone Wall Museum, opened in 2025.

1579–1632 Who Lived There
Tokugawa Hidetada
Tokugawa Hidetada (1579–1632) was the second shogun who ordered the reconstruction of Osaka Castle. He was the son of Tokugawa Ieyasu and the father of the third shogun, Tokugawa Iemitsu. The rebuilding work began in 1620 and was completed in 1629.

1627 Historical Record
Tokugawa Main Keep Completed
The Tokugawa main keep (tenshu) is completed in 1627 (Kan'ei 4) — a five-story exterior with eight interior levels, white plaster walls, and gold decorations. It is taller than Hideyoshi's original. The rebuilding of the castle as a whole was completed in 1629. The castle is used for shogunate ceremonies and as the administrative headquarters for western Japan.

1629–1665 Archaeological Record
The Castle's Dual Role
The Tokugawa-period Osaka Castle served a dual purpose: it was both a military and administrative base supporting the shogunate's rule over western Japan, and a monumental expression of shogunal power. The castle housed the Osaka-jōdai and other shogunate officials with a large contingent of samurai, and stored weapons, gunpowder, and rice.

1665–1868 What Was Lost
A Castle Without Its Tower
After the 1665 lightning fire destroyed the main keep, the castle continued to function for over 200 years without its iconic tower. The keep was never rebuilt, but Osaka Castle remained an important military and administrative base supporting the shogunate's rule over western Japan.

1620–1868 Who Lived There
The Osaka-Jodai
The Osaka-Jodai (Castle Governor) was a senior official appointed by the shogunate to oversee the castle and the administration of Osaka city. The post was in principle filled by fudai daimyo (hereditary Tokugawa vassals), and the jodai governed from the Nishinomaru area and worked alongside the Osaka machi bugyo (town magistrates).

1665 Historical Record
Lightning Destroys the Main Keep
Lightning strikes and burns down the main keep (tenshu). It is never rebuilt during the Edo period. The castle continues to function without its iconic tower for approximately 266 years.

1665–1868 Archaeological Record
The Castle Without Its Tower
The loss of the main keep did not diminish the castle's political importance. After 1665 Osaka Castle remained an important military and administrative base supporting the shogunate's rule over western Japan. Although the keep was never rebuilt, the castle governor and other officials remained in post, and samurai residences and storehouses lined the grounds.

1843 What Was Lost
Decades of Neglect
In 1843, to repair ageing buildings within the castle, the bakufu raised funds from the townspeople (yōkin) and carried out large-scale repairs. In the late-Edo period, the maintenance of Osaka Castle's buildings continued, financed in part by these merchant funds.

1867 Who Lived There
Tokugawa Yoshinobu
Tokugawa Yoshinobu (1837–1913), the 15th shogun, used Osaka Castle as an important base for shogunate governance in 1867, receiving foreign representatives there in what became an arena of late-Edo diplomacy. After the shogunate army's defeat in the Boshin War, Yoshinobu left Osaka Castle on 6 January 1868 and fled by sea toward Edo.

Modern Military Arsenal (1868–1945)
1870 Historical Record
Osaka Arsenal Established
The Osaka Arsenal is established within the castle grounds. Military leader Omura Masujiro proposed building weapons production facilities at the castle, citing its central location and excellent transport links. The arsenal expanded repeatedly, eventually becoming a massive military complex stretching from eastern Osaka Castle to the area around Morinomiya and Osaka Business Park.
Click to expand
1870–1945 Archaeological Record
From Feudal Fortress to Military Factory
The Meiji government did not preserve Osaka Castle as a former shogunal fortress; instead it placed facilities of the Ministry of Military Affairs and later the Army, using the site as a modern military base. The castle's role changed fundamentally — from an early-modern political and military centre to a military installation of the modern state. By 1945, the arsenal employed over 60,000 workers and was one of the largest military production centers in the Japanese Empire.

August 14, 1945 What Was Lost
The Final Bombing
On August 14, 1945 — the day before Japan's surrender — 145 B-29 bombers attacked the Osaka Army Arsenal. Osaka City records put the destruction at more than 80 percent of the facilities, while National Diet Library records state about 90 percent of the equipment was destroyed. The arsenal suffered catastrophic damage and ceased to function as a military factory.

1931 Who Lived There
The 1931 Keep
In 1931, the main keep was rebuilt in ferroconcrete. Of the 1.5 million yen raised by public donation, more than half went toward the Fourth Division headquarters building, with the remainder funding the keep and related castle works. This is the structure that stands today; its design drew on sources such as the "Summer Campaign at Osaka" screen and modelled the keep on the Toyotomi-period original. The 1931 reinforced-concrete keep survived the bombing, although numerous historic structures around it were destroyed.

Modern Osaka Castle (1945–Present)
1995–1997 Historical Record
Modern Restoration
The "Heisei Great Renovation" (1995–1997) repairs the exterior of the 1931 ferroconcrete keep, adds seismic reinforcement, and modernises the museum facilities. The restored castle reopens as a museum with modern interiors, including elevators.

1945–Present Who Lived There
The Castle Today
Today, Osaka Castle is no longer a residence but a museum and public park. More than 2.8 million people visit the Osaka Castle keep each year. The castle's story — from Hideyoshi's vision through the Tokugawa rebuilding to the modern restoration — is told through the stones, the walls, and the landscape itself.

The Stone Walls — A Story in Stone

The stone walls of Osaka Castle are not just defensive structures — they carry the history of powers changing hands and of a new castle built over an old one. When the Tokugawa rebuilt the castle starting in 1620, they covered the Toyotomi-period stone walls under massive quantities of earth, building entirely new walls on top.

Toyotomi Walls vs. Tokugawa Walls

Archaeological investigations show that the Toyotomi-period masonry differs markedly from the later Tokugawa walls. Surviving Toyotomi-period sections include rough-faced stone laid in nozura-zumi, differing from the cut and fitted masonry of the Tokugawa walls visible above ground today. The visible stone walls belong to the Tokugawa-period castle; Toyotomi-period walls survive beneath the later construction.

The Octopus Stone

The famous Octopus Stone (Tako-ishi) belongs to the later Tokugawa castle, not to Hideyoshi's original walls. Its enormous size illustrates the extraordinary scale of the Tokugawa reconstruction, when daimyō from across Japan were mobilised through the system of tenka-bushin to rebuild Osaka Castle.

The Toyotomi Stone Wall Museum

The Toyotomi Stone Wall Museum, opened on 1 April 2025, lets visitors see Toyotomi-period stone walls and the thickness of the Tokugawa-period earth covering them. Underground walls believed to be Toyotomi-period were first identified in 1959, and a large section was discovered beneath the honmaru in 1984. For centuries, the surviving remains of Hideyoshi's castle lay beneath the later Tokugawa construction. The walls of Osaka Castle raise the question of who gets to tell history, as physical evidence surviving in the ground.

Sources & Further Reading

The sources below form the basis of this page — Japanese-language research and historical records, together with further reading in English:

  • Shinshu Osaka-shi Shi (New Osaka City History), vol. 3 — the standard reference on Toyotomi Hideyoshi's unification, the founding of Osaka Castle, and the Toyotomi-period castle. Osaka City History Compilation Committee.
  • Shinshu Osaka-shi Shi, Historical Materials vol. 6 — source collection on Osaka Castle under the Tokugawa shogunate, the castle governor, officials, and the late-Edo castle.
  • Osaka-shi Shishi, vol. 71: Ōsaka-jō Saisei Kankei Shiryō (Materials on the Rebuilding of Osaka Castle) — key source collection for the Tokugawa rebuilding.
  • Osaka-shi Shishi, vol. 76: Ōsaka Gojin Kakki — war accounts and records of the Winter and Summer Campaigns.
  • Osaka Castle Museum, "Toyotomi Ishigaki (Stone Wall) Column" — current research on Toyotomi-period stone walls, Tokugawa-period fill, and excavation findings.
  • Osaka Castle Museum, thematic exhibition "The Rebuilding of Osaka Castle" — an account of the Tokugawa rebuilding after 1620.
  • Kansai University Naniwa/Osaka Cultural Heritage Research Center, A Newly Discovered "Toyotomi-period Osaka Castle Screen" — research on the screen held in Graz, Austria.
  • Benesch, Oleg & Zwigenberg, Ran: Japan's Castles: Architectural Innovation and the Transformation of Feudal Society (2019) — the definitive English-language study of Japanese castle architecture, including the Tokugawa rebuilding of Osaka Castle.
  • De Lange, William: Japanese Castle Explorations (2021) — comprehensive guide to Japan's castles with detailed historical analysis.
  • Hinago, Yoshiho: Japanese Castles (1986) — foundational work on castle architecture and engineering techniques.
