From Partners to “Political Problem”
Toyotomi Hideyoshi & Sen no Rikyū, 1582–1591 — Power, Recognition, and the Causes of a Rupture Still Debated

Set aside the familiar story — a ruler who loved Rikyū, then came to hate him. Recent scholarship increasingly emphasizes reading the relationship between Hideyoshi and Rikyū as something that cannot be explained by a simple opposition of “gold and wabi.” The two men were bound closely through chanoyu, but that relationship changed as Hideyoshi's political order took shape. The circumstances leading to Rikyū's punishment in 1591 remain the subject of multiple competing explanations.

Quick Answers

Hideyoshi and Rikyū cannot be captured by the simple contrast of the “lavish sovereign” and the “austere tea master.” The two men were bound closely through chanoyu, and Rikyū played an important role as Hideyoshi built his political order. As Hideyoshi constructed a new political order incorporating imperial court ranks and the control of the daimyō, however, their relationship also changed. The circumstances leading to Rikyū's punishment in 1591 remain the subject of multiple competing explanations.

Why was Sen no Rikyū killed?

Even in Japanese scholarship there is no single agreed explanation for why Rikyū was punished. In a 2011 paper, Fukui Sachio examined the sources on the circumstances and causes of Rikyū's seppuku and compared the traditional explanations. In his doctoral dissertation he further examined how later accounts of Rikyū's death were constructed. Two distinct questions remain open: what actually happened to Rikyū in 1591, and why later generations explained it the way they did.

Was the conflict really wabi-cha versus gold?

That framing is too simple. A 2019 Japanese study of Hideyoshi's and Rikyū's tea gatherings shows a shift from karamono-centered utensils toward actively adopting wamono. The practice of tea the two men shared included both lavish objects and austere ones.

Who were the four tea masters at Kitano in 1587?

The diary Kengan Kyōki (『兼見卿記』) by Yoshida Kanemi records that at the 1587 Kitano Ōchanoyu four figures stood as sadō (茶堂), in the order Kanpaku (Hideyoshi), Sōgyū (Sen no Rikyū), Sōkyū (Tsuda Sōgyū), and Sōkyū (Imai Sōkyū), and that 803 participants were counted. Rikyū was one of the central tea masters who ran the Kitano Ōchanoyu.

Was Rikyū Hideyoshi's servant?

No. Rikyū had established himself as a leading Sakai merchant and tea master before Hideyoshi rose to power. Murai Yasuhiko examined Rikyū's letters and drew attention to examples of Rikyū writing Hideyoshi's name simply as “Hideyoshi.” That form of address suggests facets of the relationship that cannot be captured by a simple lord-retainer bond. Japanese sources also note that Rikyū's residence stood among those of the major daimyō around Jurakudai.

What was the Daitokuji wooden image?

Rikyū's wooden statue was housed in the upper level of Daitokuji's Sanmon (the Kinmōkaku). When Rikyū was punished in 1591, a contemporary diary records that the statue's presence was raised as one of the issues in the matter. The statue is sometimes interpreted as symbolizing Rikyū's social and cultural standing.

Encounters & Foundations — Sakai & the Oda Regime (before 1582)
1537–1581 Command
A Peasant's Son Learning Power
Born in Nakamura in Owari, Hideyoshi entered the service of Oda Nobunaga and gradually distinguished himself within the Oda household. Much about his father Kinoshita Yazaemon — his status and origins — remains unclear, and contemporary sources for Hideyoshi's early years are limited. He showed his talent for logistics and siegecraft, building political power from below the established aristocratic and warrior elite. Before 1582 he was an emerging warrior; the ruler of Japan was still years away.

b. 1522 Recognition
A Tea Master from a Leading Sakai Merchant Family
Rikyū was born in 1522, a tea master born into a leading Sakai merchant family — a world entirely different from the battlefield. Tea gatherings connected merchants, warriors, religious figures and political elites, and the tea master could function as social intermediary and organizer of relationships. Rikyū's standing as a tea master did not depend on any lord's appointment.

Medieval Sakai Shared World
Tea Was Never a Private Hobby
Chanoyu functioned as a medium connecting social worlds that otherwise barely touched. An emerging political ruler and an already influential cultural intermediary could meet on this ground. As Hideyoshi's power expanded, Rikyū became an important figure linking the center of the regime with daimyō, courtiers and merchants through chanoyu.

Deepening — Hideyoshi's Order Takes Shape (1582–1585)
1582–1585 Command
Building the New Order
After Nobunaga's death in 1582 Hideyoshi moved with staggering speed: he defeated Akechi Mitsuhide at Yamazaki that same year, began constructing Osaka Castle the following year, 1583, and became Kanpaku in 1585. He was constructing a new political order, and needed men who could operate in worlds he had only recently entered himself.

c. 1582–1585 Recognition
From Tea Master to Confidential
Some scholarship holds that Rikyū served not only as Hideyoshi's tea master but also acted as a relay (申次, tsukisugi) between Hideyoshi and the daimyō and military lords. His relationship with Toyotomi Hidenaga — Hideyoshi's younger half-brother through a different mother — is part of this picture; Rikyū is increasingly seen as a figure who mediated the human relationships of the Toyotomi regime. The relationship had stopped being simply patron and practitioner.

The New Hierarchy Needs a Stage
Tea gatherings became one of the settings in which Hideyoshi's new political and status order could be made visible. Chanoyu & Power — The Sovereign's Cultural Policy (1585–1587)
1585–1586 Command
Kanpaku, then Daijō-daijin
Kanpaku in 1585; granted the Toyotomi surname and made Daijō-daijin in 1586. Hideyoshi's political authority through imperial court ranks grew ever more secure. The political environment in which Rikyū operated was changing extremely rapidly, and Hideyoshi's cultural performances scaled with his rank — the stage grew as the ruler grew.

1585–1587 Recognition
The Ruler's Own Tea Master
Rikyū wasn't some marginal tea instructor. He became the tea master associated with the ruler himself — his authority recognized at the very center of the new political order, at the precise moment that order was defining itself.

1587 Shared World
Kitano Ōchanoyu — Four Sadō, One Stage
Hideyoshi organized an enormous public tea gathering at Kitano Tenmangū. The diary Kengan Kyōki (兼見卿記) by Yoshida Kanemi records four figures standing as sadō (茶堂) — Kanpaku (Hideyoshi), Sōgyū (Sen no Rikyū), Sōkyū (Tsuda Sōgyū) and Sōkyū (Imai Sōkyū) — and counts 803 participants. Chanoyu was not merely a private hobby; it was also a large-scale cultural and social space in which a sovereign publicly staged authority. Rikyū was one of the central tea masters who ran the event.

1587 Scholarship
Partners Staging Power
Read this way, the mid-1580s are the opposite of confrontation: the two men were partners in constructing the meaning of Hideyoshi's power. They were not yet opposites. The "gold versus wabi" opposition of later memory had not begun to separate them — at Kitano they worked the same stage.

Political Space — Overlapping Authorities and Tension (1587–1590)
1587–1589 Command
Command Tightens
Hideyoshi increasingly determined who had the right to participate in the political order itself. As the regime grew more concerned with rank, order and political display, the space available for figures whose authority came from elsewhere — from recognition rather than decree — began to narrow.

1580s–1590 Recognition
The Political Space Around Jurakudai
As Hideyoshi's regime organized political space in Kyoto, the residences of daimyō, courtiers and figures connected to the regime were gathered around Jurakudai. Japanese historical reference material notes that Rikyū's residence was among them. Rikyū lived near this political space and through chanoyu maintained contacts with daimyō and courtiers.

Jurakudai, Kyoto Shared World
Rikyū Within the Political Space
Rikyū was not simply a man whom Hideyoshi occasionally summoned for tea. With a residence among those around Jurakudai, he was situated close to the ground where the new order displayed itself. The proximity that had bonded them now set their differences against each other.

Two Authorities Scholarship
Different Kinds of Authority
Rikyū's authority was formed by being recognized as a practitioner of chanoyu by daimyō and courtiers. Hideyoshi's, by contrast, was exercised politically through court rank, landholdings, military power and commands. The two were different in kind, yet in the 1580s they overlapped through chanoyu. As Hideyoshi's regime strengthened court rank, status and control of the daimyō, how its relationship with figures like Rikyū — who held independent networks and authority through chanoyu — would change became an important research question.

The End — The 1591 Punishment and Its Causes
1589–1591 Command
Conformity or Else
By the end of the 1580s Hideyoshi's system demanded conformity to its hierarchy. Whatever the personal chemistry had been, the relationship between two men whose authority had once complemented one another was becoming harder to reconcile within that political system. How the regime itself — advancing court rank, status and control of the daimyō — reshaped the relationship with a tea master of independent standing remains an important research question.

1591 Recognition
Punishment and a Death Still Debated
In 1591 Rikyū was punished by Hideyoshi and left Kyoto. What happened afterward remains a matter of scholarly discussion over how the sources should be read. The familiar account — an aesthetic independence that suddenly enraged the ruler — has been critically reassessed as a later popular image.

The Really Interesting Thing

The really interesting thing is not to see the two men as a simple opposition of gold and wabi. Rikyū had built an independent position in Sakai's merchant society and the world of chanoyu before he ever served Hideyoshi. Hideyoshi, meanwhile, was forming nationwide political power through court rank, military force and the control of the daimyō.

In the 1580s the two men's different kinds of authority overlapped through chanoyu. Rikyū served as Hideyoshi's tea master and is thought to have been involved in mediation with daimyō and courtiers. Then in 1591 Rikyū was punished by Hideyoshi, and scholarly debate continues over both his final days and the reasons for the punishment.

That is precisely why explaining the relationship solely as an aesthetic clash of wabi-cha against gold is not enough. What offers a handhold for understanding Hideyoshi and Rikyū is rather to look at how chanoyu, social standing and political power overlapped — and where they collided.

This timeline draws primarily on Japanese-language scholarship and Japanese institutional bibliographic and research resources. The sources below are Japanese publications or Japanese institutional records.

  • 中村修也『千利休――切腹と晩年の真実』 — 朝日新書 707, 朝日新聞出版, 2019. A study that reassesses the conventional account of Rikyū's final years through contemporary sources; an important minority interpretation questioning the standard seppuku account.
  • 趙亜男「織豊期の茶会における和物の使用と原因について――千利休と豊臣秀吉の茶会を中心に」 — 日本アジア研究 16, 埼玉大学大学院人文社会科学研究科, 2019, pp. 135–148. DOI 10.24561/00018558.

  • 『天王寺屋会記』 — Meeting records of Sakai tea practitioners (1548–1586); a primary source for Momoyama-period tea gatherings.
