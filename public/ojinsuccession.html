The Ōjin Succession
Three Records of the Same Era

The succession from Emperor Ōjin to Emperor Nintoku leaves a lasting puzzle between the story of the Nihon Shoki and the Chinese histories of the 5th century.

The Nihon Shoki draws the conflict among brothers over the throne as a story in which loyalty and self-sacrifice restore order.

The Chinese Book of Song, by contrast, records that in the 5th century the Wa kings San, Chin and others sent envoys to China’s southern dynasties, seeking recognition of their royal standing and generalships.

However, which emperors of the Nihon Shoki these kings should be identified with remains under debate.

Laying the two records together reveals a different picture of the 5th-century Wa polity and its royal successions.

Quick Answers

What was the Emperor Ōjin succession crisis?

According to the Nihon Shoki, after Emperor Ōjin’s death a conflict arose among the princes over the succession. Ōjin is said to have named his son Prince Uji no Wakiiratsuko as heir, but his elder brother Prince Ōyamamori raised troops in opposition and lost his life at the Uji River. Thereafter Uji no Wakiiratsuko and Prince Ōsazaki (the future Emperor Nintoku) repeatedly offered each other the throne; after a long interregnum, Uji no Wakiiratsuko died and Ōsazaki took the throne — so the Nihon Shoki tells it. The account can also be read as a succession tale that turns a bitter conflict among brothers into a story of loyalty and self-sacrifice. There is, however, no contemporaneous source that allows us to confirm directly what political conflict actually occurred.

Why can’t we judge from the Nihon Shoki story alone?

The Nihon Shoki was completed in 720, several centuries after the age of Ōjin and Nintoku. It is therefore not a contemporaneous record of 5th-century royal succession. Moreover, in the 5th century the Chinese history Book of Song introduces the Wa kings San, Chin, Sei, Kō and Bu. How these Five Kings of Wa correspond to the imperial line of the Nihon Shoki has been studied for a long time. San has been identified as Ōjin, Nintoku or Richū among others, and Chin as Nintoku or Hanzei among others, but nothing is settled. In short, the genealogy of the Nihon Shoki and the king names of the Book of Song cannot simply be overlaid one to one.

What do the Chinese records report?

The Book of Song records that in the 5th century the kings of Wa sent envoys to the Song dynasty of southern China. In 421 the Wa king San sent envoys, and in 425 San dispatched Sima Caoda to present tribute. Then in 438, the record says, after San died his younger brother Chin took the throne. Chin styled himself “Commissioner with Separate Credentials, Commander of Military Affairs of the six kingdoms of Wa, Baekje, Silla, Imna, Chinhan and Mokhan, Grand General who Maintains the East, and King of Wa,” and asked Song to have the title formally recognized. What Song actually recognized was “General who Maintains the East and King of Wa.” This is an important source showing that the 5th-century Wa polity sought diplomatic recognition of its position within East Asia’s international order.

Does this contradict the Nihon Shoki?

It cannot simply be declared a “contradiction.” The question is rather how to align the two records. The Nihon Shoki transmits the imperial line of Ōjin, Nintoku, Richū and Hanzei. The Book of Song transmits the names of the Wa kings San, Chin, Sei, Kō and Bu. Researchers have long tried to match them, but there are multiple theories of who San was. The National Diet Library’s overview likewise introduces theories placing San with Ōjin, Nintoku or Richū, and Chin with Nintoku or Hanzei among others. What matters here, then, is not to decide that “one of them is lying,” but to see what the two sources do and do not say about the 5th-century Wa polity.

How does this connect to Osaka?

The Nihon Shoki includes a conflict over the succession set at Naniwa around the time of Emperor Nintoku’s accession. In the reign of Emperor Richū, Nintoku’s successor, his younger brother Prince Suminoe no Ō is said to have rebelled and attacked the Naniwa Palace. Richū fled Naniwa and eventually escaped to Yamato. This story is not necessarily a verbatim record of events. But that Naniwa was remembered as an important place for the polity’s transport, logistics and politics is itself a significant archaeological question. At the northern end of the Uemachi Plateau large warehouse groups were built in the 5th century, and connections to Naniwa’s water transport are considered likely.

The Succession as the Nihon Shoki Draws It

Uji no Wakiiratsuko named heir
The Nihon Shoki says Emperor Ōjin named his son Prince Uji no Wakiiratsuko as heir. But the prince had elder brothers, and one of them — Prince Ōyamamori — opposed the decision. Worth noting is that the younger, not the elder, sons were chosen. This, however, cannot be asserted as a “deliberate provocation” or a “calculated political strategy.” What the Nihon Shoki transmits is, above all, a succession story of that shape.

Prince Ōyamamori dies — the struggle at the Uji River
Prince Ōyamamori gathered troops with the aim of attacking Uji no Wakiiratsuko, the Nihon Shoki says. But the plan was detected, and Ōyamamori was defeated and killed at the Uji River. Within the story this is drawn as the result of treason against the crown prince. Modern scholarship, however, cannot directly reconstruct what political position Ōyamamori held or what actually happened. Reading it as “an incident that eliminated a rival” is one interpretation; there is no basis for asserting outright that “the court covered up an assassination.”

421 & 425 Source
421 — King San of Wa
The Book of Song says that in 421 the Wa king San sent envoys to Song; in 425 San also dispatched Sima Caoda to present tribute. The point to note is that the mere absence of the 421 and 425 diplomatic records from the Nihon Shoki cannot be declared a deliberate suppression. The Nihon Shoki and the Book of Song are sources of different purposes, compiled at different times.

438 — King Chin of Wa
The Book of Song records that after San died his younger brother Chin took the throne. Chin sent envoys to Song and claimed for himself an official title carrying wide military authority. It included military authority over the six kingdoms of Wa, Baekje, Silla, Imna, Chinhan and Mokhan. But what Song actually granted was only part of it: General who Maintains the East and King of Wa. This matters. The 5th-century kings of Wa were not simply paying tribute to China; within East Asia’s international order they negotiated their own political and military standing.

Laying the Two Records Together
Source
Who was San?
This is one of the greatest puzzles of the Five Kings of Wa. The Book of Song transmits the king names San, Chin, Sei, Kō and Bu; the Nihon Shoki, by contrast, transmits the imperial line of Ōjin, Nintoku, Richū, Hanzei, Ingyō, Ankō and Yūryaku. Research matching the two has gone on for a long time, but there is no complete agreement. San has been identified as Ōjin, Nintoku or Richū among others; Chin as Nintoku or Hanzei among others. So either the Book of Song “misrecorded” the Japanese royal line; or the Nihon Shoki reconstructed 5th-century politics to fit a later genealogy; or the two sources are not recording the same political actors at all. With the sources we have now, it cannot simply be settled toward one of them.

Legend On Tour
Emperor Richū and Naniwa — Prince Suminoe no Ō’s rebellion
After Emperor Nintoku’s death conflict over the throne broke out again. The Nihon Shoki says that before Emperor Richū’s accession his younger brother Prince Suminoe no Ō rebelled and surrounded the Naniwa Palace. Richū escaped from Naniwa and, passing through Kawachi, eventually fled to Yamato. His younger brother Prince Mizushirowake — the future Emperor Hanzei — is said to have defeated Prince Suminoe. This was indeed a serious conflict over the succession. But to go so far as to say “it was an attempted assassination,” “a coup that nearly succeeded,” or “the shorter the entry, the more serious the event it covered up” cannot be demonstrated directly from the sources. Rather than declaring the account “suspicious,” it is historically stronger to show how the Nihon Shoki turns the rebellion into a story.

5th-Century Naniwa
5th century Archaeology On Tour
The Hōenzaka warehouse group — a polity’s logistics base
At the northern end of the Uemachi Plateau at Hōenzaka, a large warehouse group dating to the 5th century has been excavated. Six buildings in the east group and ten in the west — sixteen in all — stand in orderly rows, with an average floor area of about 90 square meters. Research evaluates them as a hub warehouse group of the Wa polity in the middle Kofun period; their position close to the mouth of the Yodo River to the north suggests a close relationship with the water transport of Naniwa. This site does not prove the succession conflict of Ōjin and Nintoku itself. But it shows that on the same 5th-century Uemachi Plateau there existed a large-scale political and logistics base capable of concentrating and managing large quantities of goods. Here is a crucial link between written sources and archaeology.

How to Read the Gaps in the Record

The Nihon Shoki and the Book of Song seem to speak of the same 5th-century Wa. Yet what they show is not the same.

The Nihon Shoki　A story drawn as: prince → succession to the throne → loyalty → treason → self-sacrifice → a legitimate accession.

The Book of Song　A record of diplomacy: king → tribute → official titles → international recognition → military designations.

The Hōenzaka warehouses　A material world: goods → water transport → concentration → management → the polity.

Laid together, the three show that the 5th-century Wa polity cannot be understood through a single story.

The Warehouses Are Still Here

Stand for a moment at the northern end of the Uemachi Plateau.

This was not only the ground of the Osaka Castle of later ages; long before that, goods gathered here through river and sea transport, and political activity unfolded. The 5th-century Hōenzaka warehouses are thought to have been part of that vast logistics system.

The Nihon Shoki told this age as a story of royal succession. The Book of Song recorded the Wa kings of the same age within diplomacy with China. And archaeology, from the warehouses and terrain remaining on the Uemachi Plateau, seeks out the scale of that politics and logistics.

Three records of the same era. None of them is the complete picture.
That is exactly why walking Naniwa has meaning.
