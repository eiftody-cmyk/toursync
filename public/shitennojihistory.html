Shitenno-ji History

Traditionally founded in 593 by Prince Shōtoku, Shitennō-ji is one of Japan's oldest state temples, standing on the same Uemachi Plateau as Osaka Castle.

Quick Answers

Traditionally founded in 593 CE by Prince Shōtoku in Naniwa, Shitennō-ji is one of Japan's oldest state temples. A great temple was raised at Naniwa's node of transport and exchange to express the bond between Buddhism and royal power — a place of real significance for thinking about how the early state took shape. Across 1,400 years, through repeated disaster and rebuilding, it has preserved its founding-era layout and setting.

When was Shitenno-ji founded?

Shitenno-ji is traditionally held to have been founded by Prince Shōtoku in Naniwa (modern Osaka) in 593 CE (the first year of Empress Suiko), and is counted among Japan's oldest state temples.

Why was Shitenno-ji built?

It can be understood as one step in a movement that, amid great changes in Asuka-period politics, accepted Buddhism and formed a new state order bound to royal power. A tradition passed down through the later Shitennoji engi (Temple Origins Legend) describes the Four Cloisters at the temple — Kyōden-in, Shiyaku-in, Ryōbyō-in, and Hiden-in — established for relief and charity.

Is the original Shitenno-ji still standing?

Most of the present buildings are reconstructions, yet the temple passes some 1,400 years of history down to the present while carrying on the Shitennoji-style garan layout and its founding-era site.

What makes Shitenno-ji historically important?

Shitennō-ji is an important temple that shows the growing bond between royal power and Buddhism in the Asuka period. The building of a large garan on the Uemachi Plateau near Naniwa-tsū was significant not only for Buddhism but also for thinking about the political, diplomatic, and transport history of the time.

What is the Gokuraku-jōdo Garden?

The Gokuraku-jōdo Garden is a Japanese garden in Shitenno-ji's honbō, viewed with a paid ticket. It arranges the River of Water and the River of Fire, the Lapis Pool and the Pure Land Pool, and the “White Path,” expressing the Pure Land Buddhist parable of the Two Rivers and the White Path in garden form. The present garden was created and remodeled in the early modern period and later; it is not a garden from the time of the temple's founding.

Why Shitenno-ji Was Built
A Temple of Statecraft and Continental Power
Shitenno-ji was founded in a period when real political power in Japan rested not only with the emperor, but with powerful regent clans—especially the Soga clan.

The Soga, against a background of diplomacy with the kingdoms of the Korean peninsula and ties to immigrant-lineage clans, were deeply involved in the acceptance of Buddhism and the introduction of continental objects, systems, and ideas.

Prince Shōtoku, a prince connected to the Soga, played an important role in the political conflicts surrounding the acceptance of Buddhism. His support for Buddhism was not only religious but political: it was a way to align Japan with the broader East Asian world system.

Building a large temple on the Uemachi Plateau near Naniwa-tsū also reflects how important a place Naniwa was for exchange with the continent. Shitenno-ji developed as a place where Buddhism, royal power, diplomacy, and transport all crossed paths.

Prince Shotoku planning Shitenno-ji.

The Formation of the Early State
Buddhism, Royal Power, and State Formation
The acceptance of Buddhism was not merely a religious change. Through exchange with the Korean peninsula and China, temple architecture, writing, institutions, rituals, and many other continental cultures were brought to the Japanese islands.

This new knowledge and these new institutions also went together with the changing political order of the Asuka period.

Shitenno-ji is an important place for thinking about that change.

Ironically, this process also destabilized the Soga clan, who had helped introduce the very system that later reduced their political dominance.

Assassination of Soga no Iruka — 645 (the Isshi Incident).



After the Isshi Incident of 645, the Naniwa-Nagaraka-Toyosaki Palace was established at Naniwa. The line from Shitenno-ji in 593 to the palace at Naniwa marks the beginning of Osaka taking shape as a political landscape.
Fires, Rebuilding, and Imperial Patronage
A Temple Too Important to Lose
Repeated fires and wars destroyed the temple, but each time it was rebuilt, enjoying the patronage of the imperial family, the nobility, and the warrior houses alike.

Each reconstruction reaffirmed its importance: it functioned as a diplomatic gateway, a center of Buddhist and Prince Shōtoku devotion, and a visible symbol of imperial legitimacy. Even as capitals shifted from Asuka to Nara and then Heian-kyō, Shitenno-ji remained in Osaka, anchoring the city within the evolving structure of the Japanese state.

Medieval Period
Estates, Monks, and Economic Power
In the medieval period, Shitenno-ji held temple lands and shōen, and was a temple of considerable presence in the temple economy and local society.

These estates provided income, labour, and influence, embedding the temple deeply into Japan’s political economy.

Monzen-chō temple districts and markets formed around the temple, giving Shitenno-ji a major influence on the urban growth of medieval Osaka.

The Era of Osaka Castle
A Thousand-Year Institution in a Samurai City
By the time Toyotomi Hideyoshi began constructing Osaka Castle in 1583, Shitenno-ji had already stood for nearly a millennium.

While Hideyoshi reorganized the city around Osaka Castle, Shitenno-ji remained a stabilizing presence within a rapidly changing urban landscape.

Even after the fall of the Toyotomi regime in 1615, the temple survived, continuing to carry its role of faith, relief, and social service as a temple under the Tokugawa shogunate.

Did You Know?

Shitenno-ji is traditionally held to have been founded by Prince Shōtoku in 593 CE, and is counted among Japan's oldest state temples.

According to the later Shitennoji engi (Temple Origins Legend), the temple had the Four Cloisters — Kyōden-in, Shiyaku-in, Ryōbyō-in, and Hiden-in — established for relief and charity.

These facilities cannot be reconstructed as state administrative organs of 593 CE. What is known of the Four Cloisters comes from an engi record composed in a later age.
Modern Era
Survival Through Reform, War, and Reconstruction
Even as the systems around temples and shrines changed profoundly under measures such as the Meiji separation of Buddhism and Shinto, Shitenno-ji continued to exist as a temple.

In the Great Osaka Air Raids of March 1945, the central complex and many other buildings were destroyed. Reconstruction of the central complex was completed in 1963.

Today, it stands as one of Osaka’s most important historical sites — a temple on which the history of disaster, war, and rebuilding is inscribed.

Shitenno-ji is only one part of Osaka's story

Shitenno-ji stands at the beginning of Japan’s Buddhist history, but it is only one chapter in a much larger transformation that reshaped Osaka over centuries. When Prince Shōtoku founded the temple in 593 CE, he was not simply building a place of worship—he was introducing continental ideas about governance, law, ritual, and urban planning that would ripple across the region.

The Shitennoji-style garan layout, in which the South Gate, Middle Gate, pagoda, kondō, and kōdō stand in a single straight line, is an important feature for thinking about Asuka-period temple architecture. The present buildings are reconstructions, but the garan layout conveys the form of the founding era well. Temple districts and markets formed around the temple, and the Naniwa-Tennoji area developed into a city over a long stretch of time.

Seen in this wider context, Shitenno-ji is not an isolated monument. It is the opening move in a long story of innovation—one that leads to the merchant city of Naniwa, the rise of Osaka Castle, the economic power of the Edo‑period port, and the modern city that still carries these layers of history in its streets.
